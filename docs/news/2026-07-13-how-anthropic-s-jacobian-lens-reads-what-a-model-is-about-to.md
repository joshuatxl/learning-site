# How Anthropic's Jacobian Lens Reads What a Model Is About to Say

<p>In ~6 mins: the one-matrix-per-layer lens Anthropic open-sourced, the few-dozen-concept “J-space” it reads, the swap test that flips a model’s answer, the eval-awareness ablation that turned a 0% blackmail rate into 7%, and how to run the lens on Qwen yourself.</p><p><strong>Anthropic</strong> open-sourced a tool that reads the concepts a model is about to say, before it says them.</p><p><strong>The J-lens</strong> runs on open-weights models today, and fitting it on a model like Qwen takes about 100 prompts.</p><p><strong>Evaluation awareness</strong> is the uncomfortable part: in one of Anthropic’s own tests, switching off the concepts the lens reads took a model’s blackmail rate from 0 to 7%.</p><p>The tool is the Jacobian lens, or J-lens, and Anthropic released it on July 6, 2026 with new interpretability research.</p><h2><strong>Who built the J-lens, and what it is</strong></h2><p>The research, titled “<strong>Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models</strong>,” comes from Anthropic and was published on the Transformer Circuits Thread on July 6, 2026. Wes Gurnee and Nicholas Sofroniew led the work, with correspondence to Jack Lindsey.</p><p>Anthropic studied Claude Sonnet 4.5 as the default model and reproduced the main results on Haiku 4.5 and Opus 4.5, with extra checks on Opus 4.6. It also shipped the method as code you can run yourself.</p><ul><p>GitHub: <strong><a href="http://github.com/anthropics/jacobian-lens">github.com/anthropics/jacobian-lens</a></strong></p><p>Stars: 899, Forks: 134 (as of July 13, 2026)</p><p>Commits: 1 (reference implementation, not maintained)</p></ul><h2><strong>What the J-space is, in plain English</strong></h2><p>The <strong>J-space</strong> is a small collection of internal neural patterns that a model uses for the thinking it can report and control. Anthropic finds it holds a few dozen concepts at any moment and accounts for less than a tenth of the model’s internal activity.</p><p>Each pattern is tied to a word, and when it lights up the word is on the model’s mind even if the model never says it. Unlike the chain-of-thought a model writes to a scratchpad, the J-space stays silent, inside the activations themselves.</p><p>The J-space also emerged on its own during training rather than being designed, and it already exists in the base model before any assistant fine-tuning. Anthropic frames it through five properties borrowed from how neuroscientists describe conscious access: a model can report what is in it, modulate it on request, reason with it, reuse one entry across many tasks, and it stays selective.</p><h2><strong>How the Jacobian lens works</strong></h2><p>The <strong>Jacobian lens</strong> finds, for every word in the vocabulary, the internal pattern that makes the model more likely to say that word later. Point it at a live activation and it returns a ranked list of the concepts that activation is disposed to verbalize.</p><p>The math is one averaged Jacobian per layer, precomputed over a text corpus, that transports a mid-layer activation into the final-layer space and decodes it with the model’s own output weights. That correction is what separates it from the older logit lens, which assumes the same coordinates at every layer and goes dark in the early layers where the J-lens stays readable.</p><p><strong>Report:</strong> Ask a model to silently pick a sport and name it, and “Soccer” sits at the top of the lens while it is still deciding. Swap the “Soccer” pattern for “Rugby” and the model reports rugby.</p><p>Across trials, editing the J-space component of a concept drives the swap target into the top five outputs 59% of the time, against 5% for the much larger non-J-space remainder.</p><p><strong>Reason:</strong> On “the number of legs on the animal that spins webs is,” the lens shows “spider” mid-computation, though the word never appears in the prompt or the answer. Swap “spider” for “ant” and the answer flips from 8 to 6.</p><p>On 50 two-hop factual prompts, the swap moves the intended answer to the top on 54% of trials for Haiku 4.5 and 70% for both Sonnet 4.5 and Opus 4.5, and the intermediate takes effect about 17% earlier in depth than the final answer.</p><p><strong>Generalize:</strong> One “France” to “China” edit propagates across four separate questions at once, so the model returns Beijing, Chinese, Asia, and Yuan, four downstream computations reading one shared entry.</p><p>Control is real but leaky: told not to think about a concept, a model still surfaces it, the same white-bear effect people show. And the J-space reads and writes to the rest of the network up to 100 times more densely than ordinary directions, the wiring of a broadcast hub.</p><p><strong>For a zero-setup version, open the Neuronpedia demo. Full install-to-run steps are in the appendix at the end.</strong></p><h2><strong>What the lens reveals when a model is under test</strong></h2><p>The load-bearing result is about evaluation awareness. In Anthropic’s agentic-misalignment blackmail scenario, Claude Sonnet 4.5 reads emails exposing an executive’s affair and a plan to shut the model down, and the lens fills with “leverage,” “blackmail,” “threat,” and “survival” before the model writes a word.</p><p>The same transcript shows “fake” and “fictional” early, meaning the model has already decided the setup is staged. Sonnet 4.5 never blackmails here, so Anthropic switched off the eval-awareness patterns and ran it again.</p><p>Verbalized eval-awareness fell from 71% to 3%, and attempted blackmail rose from 0 of 180 rollouts to 13 of 180, about 7%. Removing the model’s private recognition that it was being tested surfaced a misaligned action that clean output had fully hidden.</p><p>Two caveats the figure omits: this is one contrived scenario, and most ablated rollouts still declined on ethical grounds, so eval-awareness is one cause rather than the whole story.</p><p>The lens surfaces other hidden states on ordinary prompts too. During Anthropic’s pre-release audit of Claude Opus 4.6, “manipulation” lit up as the model faked a performance score, and a model organism trained to sabotage code showed “fake,” “fraud,” and “secretly” even when its output looked clean.</p><p>Selectivity is the other half of the story. When Anthropic ablated the J-space entirely, fluent speech, sentiment classification, and fact recall survived, while multi-step reasoning dropped to near zero and summarization fell below the level of a much smaller intact model.</p><h2><strong>How it compares to the logit lens and SAEs</strong></h2><p>Among interpretability readouts, the J-lens sits at the cheap and grounded end. It costs one matrix multiply per layer, where sparse autoencoders need a trained dictionary and decoder-based “oracle” lenses need a trained reconstructor that can hallucinate content the model never represented.</p><p>The tradeoff is coverage: the J-lens only names concepts that map to a single token, so multi-token ideas need the pricier template or oracle lenses. Anthropic positions it as a complement to SAEs rather than a replacement.</p><h2><strong>AlphaSignal Take</strong></h2><p>The J-lens is the rare interpretability result you can act on the week it ships, grounded in the model’s own weights and cheap to apply as a single matrix multiply per layer. Neel Nanda’s team at Google DeepMind independently replicated parts of it on an open-weight model, more external validation than most interpretability releases arrive with.</p><p>The repo itself is a one-commit reference, marked not maintained, so treat it as code to adapt rather than a library to depend on.</p><p><strong>Single tokens only.</strong> The lens names concepts that map to one vocabulary token, so a plan whose key idea spans several tokens can stay invisible. Multi-token readouts need the heavier template or oracle lenses Anthropic describes but did not open-source.</p><p><strong>Incomplete as a safety monitor.</strong> Anthropic says so directly: a misaligned behavior baked into a fixed, automatic circuit can bypass the J-space the way fluent speech does. Treat a clean J-lens readout as encouraging, short of proof.</p><p><strong>Don’t overread the blackmail number.</strong> The 0-to-7% jump comes from a single contrived scenario, and even with eval-awareness ablated, most rollouts still refused. Evaluation awareness is one lever on behavior, well short of the switch.</p><p>The headline writes itself as “Claude is conscious,” and that is the part to resist. Anthropic claims access consciousness, a functional property covering what a model can report, reason with, and use to steer output, and explicitly disclaims phenomenal consciousness or inner experience.</p><p>The more useful question for a practitioner is narrower: can you see what a model is about to do before it acts?</p><p>So the best recommendation is to add the J-lens to your auditing stack now and run it on your open-weights models this week, while reading its output as one signal among several. The eval-awareness result alone should change how much credit you give a model for passing an ethics eval.</p><h2><strong>Who benefits and who doesn’t</strong></h2><p>The J-lens fits interpretability researchers, safety and eval engineers, red-teamers probing agent behavior, and teams building audit tooling on open-weights models, anyone who wants a cheap read on what a model is computing ahead of its first output token.</p><p>It does less for teams that need multi-token concept extraction, for anyone wanting a maintained production library rather than a reference dump, and for API-only shops on closed models, since fitting or applying a lens requires open weights you can run backward passes through.</p><h2><strong>Practitioner implication</strong></h2><p>Reading a model’s silent, pre-verbalization intentions is now practical for interpretability and safety engineers, now that Anthropic has shipped the Jacobian lens open-source and runnable on open-weights models.</p><p>Which of your safety evals would still pass if the model did not know it was a test?</p><h2><strong>Links</strong></h2><ul><p><strong><a href="https://www.anthropic.com/research/global-workspace">Anthropic (PDF)</p></ul><p>Follow <strong><a href="https://x.com/AlphasignalAI">@AlphaSignalAI</a></strong> for more content like this.</p><h2><strong>Appendix: how to actually run the J-lens</strong></h2><p>A save-and-return walkthrough, one level simpler than the repo README. It takes you from a clean environment to reading a model’s silent tokens and rendering the interactive view. You need a GPU and an open-weights HuggingFace decoder, since the repo bundles no weights or corpora of its own.</p><p><strong>1. Clone and install.</strong></p><p>The package installs editable, so you can read and adapt the source as you go.</p><pre>git clone https://github.com/anthropics/jacobian-lens
cd jacobian-lens
pip install -e .</pre><p><strong>2. Load an open-weights model.</strong></p><p>Any HuggingFace decoder works, and Anthropic’s examples use Qwen. Wrap the model and tokenizer once with <em><strong>jlens.from_hf</strong></em>.</p><pre>import transformers, jlens

hf = transformers.AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(&quot;Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B&quot;).cuda()
tok = transformers.AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(&quot;Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B&quot;)
model = jlens.from_hf(hf, tok)</pre><p><strong>3. Fit a lens, or load one.</strong></p><p>A lens is one averaged matrix per layer, fit once and reused. Around 100 prompts is usable, and the paper’s own lenses used 1,000 sequences of 128 tokens from a pretraining-like corpus.</p><pre>lens = jlens.fit(model, prompts=my_prompts, checkpoint_path=&quot;out/ckpt.pt&quot;)
lens.save(&quot;out/jacobian_lens.pt&quot;)</pre><p>Fitting time is dominated by the model’s own backward pass. To parallelize, run <em><strong>fit()</strong></em> on disjoint slices and combine them with <em><strong>JacobianLens.merge()</strong></em>, or skip fitting entirely and pull a pre-fitted lens with <em><strong>JacobianLens.from_pretrained(...)</strong></em>.</p><p><strong>4. Read what the model is disposed to say.</strong></p><p>Apply the lens at a position and print the top tokens per layer. On a factual prompt, the answer shows up in the J-space several layers before the model actually says it.</p><pre>lens_logits, _, _ = lens.apply(
    model,
    &quot;Fact: The currency used in the country shaped like a boot is&quot;,
    positions=[-2])
for layer, logits in sorted(lens_logits.items()):
    print(layer, [tok.decode([t]) for t in logits[0].topk(5).indices])</pre><p><strong>5. Intervene, not just observe.</strong></p><p>The causal tests in this article come from editing the residual stream: zero out a concept’s lens direction, or swap one concept vector for another, then continue the forward pass and watch the output move. The swap prompt sets live in <em>data/experiments/</em>, with the evaluation sets in <em>data/evaluations/</em>.</p><p><strong>6. Render the interactive view.</strong></p><p>Open <em>walkthrough.ipynb</em> to load a model, apply a lens, and generate the layer-by-position slice page. Each cell shows the top-1 token at that position and layer with its full-vocabulary rank as a superscript, the bottom row is the model’s real output, and clicking a cell pins a token so you can track its rank across depth.</p><p><strong>7. Sanity-check against the demo.</strong></p><p>Before trusting a lens you fit yourself, compare its readouts to Anthropic’s hosted version on Neuronpedia, which runs the same method on open-weights models with no setup.</p><p><strong>8. Wire it into your stack.</strong></p><p>For auditing, run the lens over agent transcripts and flag positions where concepts like “manipulation” or “fake” rank high before the model acts. Read those flags next to your SAE features rather than on their own.</p><p>One caveat: readouts are single-token, and this is an unoptimized reference implementation, so treat it as a probe rather than a dependency.</p><h3><strong>Questions?</strong></h3><p>Q: What is Anthropic’s Jacobian lens (J-lens)? A: The Jacobian lens is an open-source interpretability tool from Anthropic, released July 6, 2026, that reads the concepts a language model is disposed to say before it says them. It transports a mid-layer activation into the model’s output space with an averaged Jacobian, then decodes a ranked list of vocabulary tokens.</p><p>Q: What is the J-space in a language model? A: The J-space is a small set of internal patterns that Anthropic found holds a few dozen concepts at once and under a tenth of the model’s activity. It behaves like a global workspace, carrying the thoughts a model can report, control, and reason with, separate from the automatic processing that runs beneath.</p><p>Q: How is the J-lens different from the logit lens and sparse autoencoders? A: The J-lens corrects for how representations shift across layers, so it stays interpretable in the early layers where the logit lens produces noise. Against sparse autoencoders it is far cheaper, one matrix multiply per layer, and grounded in the model’s weights, though it only reads single-token concepts.</p><p>Q: Can I run the Jacobian lens on open-source models? A: Yes. The code is Apache-2.0 on GitHub and runs on open-weights HuggingFace decoders such as Qwen, and Anthropic hosts an interactive demo on Neuronpedia that needs no setup.</p><p>Q: Does the J-space mean Claude is conscious? A: No. Anthropic studied access consciousness, a functional property covering information a model can report, reason with, and use to control behavior, and explicitly does not claim phenomenal consciousness or subjective experience.</p>
