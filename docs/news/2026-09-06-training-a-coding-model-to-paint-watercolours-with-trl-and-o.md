# Training a coding model to paint watercolours with TRL and OpenEnv

<h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#training-a-coding-model-to-paint-watercolours-with-trl-and-openenv"> </a> Training a coding model to paint watercolours with TRL and OpenEnv</h2><p>On 23 August, <a href="https://x.com/kickingkeys/status/2091570990048276897">Surya Narreddi</a> posted a beautiful video of watercolours painted by a language model. The model writes JavaScript through <a href="https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush">p5.brush</a>, a library that &quot;adds natural drawing tools to p5.js&quot;. The video went viral fast, over 1.5M views at the time of writing.</p><p>The video came with <a href="https://surya.website/rling-qwen-to-paint-with-code">a blog post</a> explaining the training behind an earlier and narrower stage of the project, close-up flowers rather than the full compositions in the video, sadly without open artifacts yet. His site says a full technical report is coming, so ensure you follow him. The original idea is his, coming from the art and design side, where <a href="https://x.com/kickingkeys/status/2094901433149612118">his skills are way beyond mine</a>. My attempt is on the engineering side, reproducing the recipe in the open with every piece published.</p><p><strong>Note:</strong> for the context behind the project, told by Surya himself, watch <a href="https://vimeo.com/1190839818">this video of his thesis</a>.</p><p>In this article I try and reproduce his idea with <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/trl">TRL</a> and <a href="https://github.com/huggingface/OpenEnv">OpenEnv</a>. The reference pool dataset, the RL environment, the training scripts and the trained models, all open.</p><p>The whole pipeline runs on Hugging Face, end to end:</p><ul><li>training on <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/huggingface_hub/guides/jobs">Jobs</a></li><li>the RL environment and the scorer model as <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/spaces">Spaces</a></li><li>the pairwise judge through <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/inference-providers">Inference Providers</a></li><li>and every artifact on the Hub, gathered in <a href="https://huggingface.co/collections/HuggingEnvs/paint-with-code-6a955b79d63f67f1631d9be6">one collection</a></li></ul><p>Once the two Spaces are up, the recipe is one command. Duplicate the <a href="https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingEnvs/watercolour-env">environment</a> and the <a href="https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingEnvs/watercolour-hpsv3">scorer model</a>, set two environment variables for the reward mix, and launch:</p><pre>hf jobs uv run train/watercolour_grpo.py --flavor h200 --timeout 48h --secrets HF_TOKEN -- \
  --env-url https://-watercolour-env.hf.space \
  --model Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B --lora --all-linear --bf16 --gradient-checkpointing \
  --subject &#x27;a peach hibiscus&#x27; --references 4 \
  --top-p 0.95 --top-k 20 \
  --lr 5e-5 --lr-scheduler constant_with_warmup --warmup-steps 5 \
  --scale-rewards none \
  --steps 110 --n-episodes 240 --num-generations 8 \
  --per-device-batch-size 1 --gradient-accumulation-steps 8 \
  --max-completion-length 8192 \
  --run-tag my-run --out /watercolour-grpo --push-to-hub</pre><p>The rest of this article is the story of getting there, and <a href="https://github.com/adithya-s-k/HuggingEnvs/tree/main/02-watercolour">every piece is in the repo</a>.</p><p>I have followed the original blog step by step, and only changed something when strictly needed. Every idea of my own went into a list instead of into the experiment, and that list became &quot;What I would try next&quot; at the end, next to the full list of published artifacts. If you have already read his post, the framing and the reward design will be familiar. The new material is the open implementation, the hand-rated pool, and three reward mixes trained and compared, and it starts at <a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#the-rl-environment-you-need-to-build">The RL environment you need to build</a>.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#why-people-loved-it"> </a> Why people loved it</h2><p>The paintings look loose, imperfect, handmade, at a moment when image models produce perfect (statistically average) pictures. My guess is that this contrast is a big part of why the video went viral. It reminded me of the early days of generative AI art, when the point was to explore the medium. <a href="https://research.google/blog/inceptionism-going-deeper-into-neural-networks/">DeepDream</a> (2015) was a debugging tool that people turned into art, works like <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_de_Belamy">Edmond de Belamy</a> (2018) came from artists probing what a GAN could do, and artists like <a href="https://quasimondo.com">Mario Klingemann</a> spent those years making <a href="https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/memories-of-passerby-i-mario-klingemann/aAHG7iV3aXme8g">dreamy portraits with neural networks</a>.</p><p>This project feels closer to those early days. In his thesis, Surya describes the path that led here. He started by prompting text-to-image models, where the prompt is the only lever you can pull, and more detail buys more control only up to a point. Training the model itself goes further. The other half of the idea is the medium. The model writes a program of about 150 lines of JavaScript that paints the image. That model output is code. You can read it, edit it and run it again, and the decision behind each brushstroke is visible. And the style comes from a restriction where the model is only allowed <em>ten of the library&#x27;s methods</em>. More on that below.</p><p>In that same period, <a href="https://annaridler.com/works/myriad-tulips">Anna Ridler</a> photographed thousands of tulips, hand-labelled every one, exhibited the dataset itself as the artwork, and later trained a model on it. I found her work through the references AI agents brought back while building this project and loved it because this project does something very similar by curating a set of images by hand, and then training against them.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#rl-over-taste"> </a> RL over taste</h2><p>Most of the recent RL work on language models uses rewards you can verify. For example, math problems with a known answer, code that passes tests, or graders that are right or wrong and cheap to run. This project is closer to the older exception, RLHF, where the model learns a reward model from human preferences.</p><p>Here the reward is aesthetic preference. There is no <em>correct</em> answer. The real question of the project is whether you can do RL over taste.</p><p>The reward, as his blog defines it and as the RL environment I built implements it:</p><p><a href="https://huggingface.co/MizzenAI/HPSv3">HPSv3</a> is an open 7B preference model. Give it an image and a text description, and it returns a score for how much a person would prefer that image. It was trained on a large set of human choices between pairs of images, so its score is an average of many people&#x27;s taste. The pairwise judge is <a href="https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct">Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B-Instruct</a>, a general vision model called through HF Inference Providers. The pairwise judge sees the candidate painting next to four references randomly selected from the pool, guided by a written description of what to weigh (bleeds, translucent washes, soft edges), each comparison in both presentation orders, and its score is the share of comparisons the candidate wins. Its only standard is the pool, so its score is my taste, as encoded in those ratings.</p><p>Those are the weights Narreddi converged on. The pool defines taste here. That moves the work from tuning hyperparameters to building the set that decides what is beautiful.</p><p>I trained three runs with this reward. They differ only in how the weight splits between the two model judges:</p><p>I started with <code>hps-only</code> to validate that the pipeline could learn at all. Once the reward was going up and the metrics were healthy, there was no reason to run it longer, so I launched the two longer runs instead. The question that the longer runs ask is how much of HPSv3&#x27;s power can you hand to the pairwise judge? The more weight the judge carries, the more the reward means <em>my</em> taste instead of everyone&#x27;s, and the harder it should be to climb. Incidentally, if you push it far enough or your style is too far from the average, the model could stop entirely.</p><p>Fortunately, it did not stop and both runs with the pairwise judge on learned too. The hand-rated pool can steer the policy, at least as far as the metrics and the final paintings show. The numbers are below.</p><p><strong>Disclaimer.</strong> If we use a frontier model, it can already generate the JavaScript code that paints watercolour from a prompt. That&#x27;s the starting point. The work here is about teaching a smaller model to do it combined with a person&#x27;s own artistic preferences.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#the-rl-environment-you-need-to-build"> </a> The RL environment you need to build</h2><p>The environment wraps everything that sits between the model and the reward, including the JavaScript library that the model uses for painting, the system prompt that restricts it, the headless Chromium that renders each sketch, and the gate that rejects cheats.</p><p>The library does more of the work than it seems. <a href="https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush">p5.brush</a>, by <a href="https://x.com/acamposuribe">@acamposuribe</a>, simulates a medium rather than drawing shapes: pigment bleeds past the edges of a fill, paper has texture, strokes have mass, flow fields drag brushwork around. When the model calls <code>brush.fillBleed(0.25)</code> it is deciding how far the ink runs.</p><p><strong>Note.</strong> The author of p5.brush had been trying to teach a machine to paint long before any of this. In 2022 he made a generative art series that hides a diary about teaching p5.js to draw like a child: <em>&quot;It is barely able to use the crayons [...] It cannot follow simple commands. I&#x27;m done for today, very infuriating.&quot;</em> The series was meant to have three pieces, and he made two. When Surya&#x27;s video went viral, <a href="https://x.com/acamposuribe/status/2091668313449316651">he quoted it</a>, sharing that diary and saying this work is the third piece arriving on its own.</p><p>p5.brush exposes 47 methods. The prompt allows 10: <code>scaleBrushes</code>, <code>noStroke</code>, <code>fill</code>, <code>noFill</code>, <code>fillBleed</code>, <code>fillTexture</code>, <code>beginShape</code>, <code>vertex</code>, <code>endShape</code> and <code>circle</code>. What the other thirty-seven add, lines, hatching, custom brushes, would break the watercolour look. With these ten the model can only paint filled shapes, and the library adds the bleed to every one of them.</p><p>His blog post saved me a lot of time I could have wasted iterating on the prompt. A long API reference makes the model invent methods that do not exist, and his 200 GEPA iterations converged on a strict allowlist with no documentation. I saw the same failures and wrote the allowlist by hand. My only addition to that recipe is one sentence: paint each petal two or three times, a big pass first and a smaller, more opaque one inside it. This small change made my outputs a lot more colorful.</p><p><strong>Note.</strong> If it&#x27;s the first time you hear about <a href="https://huggingface.co/papers/2507.19457">GEPA</a>, it is an automatic prompt optimizer. A language model reflects in plain words on where the current prompt failed and proposes a better one, and the loop repeats.</p><p>The gate is the final piece. The sketch has to compile, use the library instead of direct p5 calls, put real pigment on the canvas, and not try to trick the scorer, for example by writing text on the canvas.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#the-pool-is-the-reward-function"> </a> The pool is the reward function</h2><p>The pool consists of <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingEnvs/watercolour-reference-pool">178 paintings</a> divided into two tiers based on my personal preferences, <code>love</code> and <code>okay</code>. All of them are actually generated by a model. Four open-weight models, called through Inference Providers, wrote p5.brush sketches, each one working from a real, openly licensed photo of a hibiscus from iNaturalist. A vision model gave written feedback on every sketch, over three refinement iterations. Every final render was then rated one at a time, by me, and 178 made the cut.</p><p>Here I chose four different families of models to test their different styles. These four were the open models that produced a valid sketch every time in a quick reliability check, and two other candidates were dropped for failing it. If you want to produce your own pool, you might choose others.</p><p>The tiers do real work in the reward. When the pairwise judge draws four references, half come from <code>love</code> and half from <code>okay</code>, so the policy always faces some rivals it can sometimes beat, and a win pays the same against either tier. This is one of my few deliberate changes: the original compares against its top tier only, and I kept the easier tier in the draw so a weak early policy still gets signal.</p><p>No human-made painting is in there, which is a real limitation. p5.brush is a niche library, and the human work that exists in it with accessible code is a handful of pieces, nowhere near what a training corpus would need, as his blog also notes.</p><p>The interesting idea here, as I&#x27;ve already discussed previously, is that the model will learn to imitate what the pool contains. If we point the environment at a different dataset, the reward would automatically change without touching a single line of code. For the dataset I generated and openly share, I also include the source sketch.</p><p>If we look closer at the judges, the two of them answer different questions. HPSv3 decides whether it is a flower, and the pairwise judge decides whether it is well painted in the style I chose.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#just-one-more-yolo-run"> </a> Just one more yolo run</h2><p>Before anything worked, there was a long stretch of flat reward curves. If you&#x27;ve tried to reproduce a research paper/blog without open artifacts, you probably can relate. Every run tested what I thought was a reasonable theory about what was wrong. A run takes a lot of time, so I queued the next one while still going through the last one&#x27;s results. As always, starting from something easier that works and then building on top of that was the answer. A simple control task, with no browser and no judges, was the first attempt that learned, and the reason was that my learning rate was just too low.</p><p>Another change that cost me time to find was adjusting the LoRA parameters correctly. The usual <code>target_modules</code> list assumes a dense model, and <a href="https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B"><code>Qwen/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B</code></a> is a mixture of experts that names most of its projections differently, so the adapter was training ten layers out of forty. I solved this by changing it to <code>all-linear</code>, which reaches every linear layer. The routed experts in this architecture are fused tensors that even <code>all-linear</code> leaves frozen, but everything else gets an adapter, and that was enough to learn.</p><p>The fix was four changes in TRL&#x27;s <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/grpo_trainer"><code>GRPOTrainer</code></a>:</p><p>These four changes unlocked the first successful run (<code>hps-only</code>), with the reward clearly improving.</p><p>With that configuration, all three runs learn. Both judge runs were launched for 200 steps and stopped at 110, with the reward still climbing slowly. A step takes fifteen to eighteen minutes, and the comparison between mixes was already stable, so I stopped both to save compute. Mean group reward over the first and final third of each run:</p><p>The three curves line up with how much weight my taste carries. The more the judge weighs, the lower the start and the noisier the climb. <code>judge-led</code> spent its first thirty steps nearly flat before it moved. It is the same move that solved the debugging. Shrink the problem until something learns, then add the hard parts back one at a time.</p><p>The pairwise judge term itself climbed in the two runs that used it. The model wins more comparisons against the pool as training advances, which is the claim <code>hps-only</code> could not make. No group in any run collapsed to identical rewards, the GRPO failure mode that kills the gradient. For the curious, the per-metric curves (HPSv3, paint coverage, entropy) are <a href="https://github.com/adithya-s-k/HuggingEnvs/tree/main/02-watercolour/results">in the repository as CSV</a>.</p><p>The full launch command, the hardware and the two environment variables that turn this into the other two runs are in <a href="https://github.com/adithya-s-k/HuggingEnvs/tree/main/02-watercolour">the recipe</a>.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-to-paint-with-code#what-it-actually-learned"> </a> What it actually learned</h2><p><strong>In every run, the first thing the model learned was to stop producing bad paintings</strong>, the near-blank canvases and shapeless washes that score under 0.3 in total reward. In <code>hps-only</code>, three quarters of the rise in the group mean comes from bad paintings becoming rare. In the judge runs the collapse is even steeper: rollouts under 0.3 fall from 99 to 16 across <code>judge-led</code>&#x27;s thirds, and from 37 to 4 in <code>hps-led</code>.</p><p>This is why the obvious visual, the best painting of each step, shows almost no difference in <code>hps-only</code>. It moves <strong>+0.034</strong> across the run while the median moves <strong>+0.155</strong>. The learning is visible in the middle of the distribution.</p><p><strong>What the pairwise judge changes is the top.</strong> In <code>hps-only</code>, paintings got more reliable without getting better. The quality of the good ones added just +0.03 to the group mean, and once HPSv3 saw petals around a centre and a stem, it stopped asking for more pigment. With the judge on, the other half of the story appears. <em>Better</em> here means closer to the pool, so closer to what I rated as &quot;good&quot; or something I liked more. That added +0.12 in <code>judge-led</code> and +0.16 in <code>hps-led</code>, the best of each step rose too, and paint coverage doubled in both runs (0.11 to 0.23, and 0.13 to 0.30) where <code>hps-only</code> barely moved it. With a reference left to beat, a good painting can still get better, and the model starts being rewarded for using more pigment.</p><p>One more finding. The model ignores an explicit instruction, and it is right to. The system prompt asks for fifteen to thirty filled shapes. If we look at the real mean, it is between 7 and 9, and <code>n_shapes</code> barely correlates with reward in any run (+0.000, −0.14, +0.07). The policy is not rewarded for obeying that sentence, so it does not obey it.</p><p>There is also a ceiling on the <code>hps-only</code> route. If every rollout matched its good ones, that run&#x27;s mean would sit at 0.771. Whether more steps would break it is an open question.
