href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#what-are-multi-vector-models"> </a> What are Multi-Vector models?</h2><p>A dense embedding model compresses a whole text into a single vector, and similarity is one dot product between two such summaries. A multi-vector model (also called a late-interaction or ColBERT-style model) skips that compression. It keeps <strong>one small vector per token</strong> and scores a query against a document with the MaxSim operator, where every query token finds its best-matching document token and the scores are summed. Token-level matching preserves exactly the fine-grained signals that a single vector has to average away, which usually means stronger retrieval, at the cost of a bigger index.</p><p>The companion <a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/multi-vector-encoder">Multi-Vector Embedding Models</a> blogpost covers the architecture, encoding, scoring, and indexing in detail, so I&#x27;ll keep this section short and get to the training.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#why-finetune"> </a> Why Finetune?</h2><p>Finetuning multi-vector models significantly improves their retrieval performance on your specific domain: the vocabulary, the query style, and the notion of relevance all differ between web search, legal discovery, code search, and scientific literature review. Because queries and documents are matched token by token, multi-vector models pick up fine-grained domain signals that single-vector models tend to average away, and they respond very well to even modest amounts of in-domain finetuning data.</p><p>Beyond that, most released retrieval models were configured for short passages. The classic ColBERT checkpoints truncate documents at 180 or 300 tokens, and many popular dense models at 256 or 512, because their MS MARCO-style training data rarely goes beyond that. If your documents are long, these models silently discard most of every document before scoring it. On my medical evaluation with passages averaging 941 tokens, I measured that this truncation costs up to 0.24 NDCG@10, considerably more than any difference between model architectures. When you train your own model, you configure the document length that <em>your</em> data needs.</p><p>LightOn ran into this same dynamic with code retrieval, where general <a href="https://huggingface.co/lightonai/LateOn">LateOn</a> wasn&#x27;t enough and they trained <a href="https://huggingface.co/lightonai/LateOn-Code">LateOn-Code</a>. Your domain, whether that&#x27;s medical, legal, financial, or your company&#x27;s internal documents, is not getting an official model. This blogpost shows you how to build it yourself, in a matter of hours, on a single consumer GPU.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#training-components"> </a> Training Components</h2><p>Training MultiVectorEncoder models involves the following components:</p><ol><li><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#model"><strong>Model</strong></a>: The model to finetune or the architecture to build fresh.</li><li><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#dataset"><strong>Dataset</strong></a>: The data used for training and evaluation.</li><li><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#loss-function"><strong>Loss Function</strong></a>: A function that measures the model&#x27;s performance and guides the optimization process.</li><li><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#training-arguments"><strong>Training Arguments</strong></a> (optional): Parameters that impact training performance, tracking, and debugging.</li><li><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#evaluator"><strong>Evaluator</strong></a> (optional): A class for evaluating the model before, during, or after training.</li><li><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#trainer"><strong>Trainer</strong></a>: Brings together all training components.</li></ol><p>Let&#x27;s take a closer look at each component.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#model"> </a> Model</h2><p>Multi-vector training gives you a real choice of starting point, and it matters more than you might expect.</p><h3><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#finetuning-an-existing-multi-vector-model"> </a> Finetuning an existing multi-vector model</h3><p>If you want to further finetune an existing multi-vector model, you don&#x27;t have to worry about the architecture at all:</p><pre>from sentence_transformers import MultiVectorEncoder


model = MultiVectorEncoder(
    &quot;lightonai/mLateOn-unsupervised&quot;,
    model_kwargs={&quot;torch_dtype&quot;: &quot;float32&quot;},
    processor_kwargs={&quot;model_max_length&quot;: 8192},
)</pre><p>The checkpoint brings its own recipe along: its query and document marker tokens, its projection head, its scoring skiplist. For finetuning, you generally want to keep all of that and change only what your data demands. The first thing to check is the length configuration, since many released checkpoints cap documents at 180 to 512 tokens (see <a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#why-finetune">Why Finetune?</a>), and my medical passages run to 1,400 tokens. The mLateOn family already serves the backbone&#x27;s full 8192 token context, but if your starting checkpoint carries caps, lift them:</p><pre>model[0].query_length = None
model[0].document_length = None</pre><p>With the per-task caps unset, truncation falls back to the tokenizer&#x27;s <code>model_max_length</code>, which is why I configure that limit at load time above.</p><p>I made one more change, adding a punctuation skiplist that excludes punctuation tokens from document-side scoring and storage. In a 4-way ablation (none, punctuation, stopwords, both) it modestly won on quality, and it shrinks the document index by 9.6% on this data for free:</p><pre>import string


model[2].skiplist_words = list(string.punctuation)
model[2].resolve_with_tokenizer(model.tokenizer)</pre><h3><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#building-one-from-a-base-transformer"> </a> Building one from a base transformer</h3><p>You can also point <code>MultiVectorEncoder</code> at any base transformer, and a fresh, randomly initialized token-level projection is appended for you:</p><pre>from sentence_transformers import MultiVectorEncoder

model = MultiVectorEncoder(&quot;answerdotai/ModernBERT-base&quot;, model_kwargs={&quot;torch_dtype&quot;: &quot;float32&quot;})</pre><p>That&#x27;s the classic ColBERT pipeline: a <code>Transformer</code> producing contextualized token embeddings, a token-level <code>Dense</code> projecting each of them down to 128 dimensions, a <code>MultiVectorMask</code> deciding which tokens count during scoring, and a token-level <code>Normalize</code>. The projection starts random, so training is required before this model is useful. Interestingly, this works with strong dense embedding backbones too. A fresh projection on <a href="https://huggingface.co/Alibaba-NLP/gte-modernbert-base">Alibaba-NLP/gte-modernbert-base</a> reached within 0.03 of the existing-checkpoint starting points in my experiments, from nothing but the projection and 25k training pairs.</p><p>The classic ColBERT tokenization tricks (<code>[MASK]</code> query expansion, <code>[Q]</code> / <code>[D]</code> prefix tokens, a document length cap, a punctuation skiplist) are all off by default and configurable. See <a href="https://sbert.net/docs/multi_vector_encoder/usage/custom_models.html">Creating Custom Models</a> for the full set. For what it&#x27;s worth, I tested <code>[MASK]</code> query expansion in four configurations for my domain finetune and none of them made a measurable difference, so don&#x27;t feel obliged to reach for the classic recipe.</p><h3><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#which-starting-point-should-you-pick"> </a> Which starting point should you pick?</h3><p>I measured this directly while preparing this blogpost, taking six starting points and training each with the identical recipe on 25k medical question-passage pairs from <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/tomaarsen/miriad-4.4M-split">MIRIAD</a>, then evaluating on 1,000 held-out questions against a 50,000 passage corpus:</p><p>The result surprised me, and it replicated across two model families. *The <code>-unsupervised</code> checkpoints adapt to a new domain far better than their finished siblings, overtaking them despite starting lower. These checkpoints sit after large-scale contrastive pretraining but before supervised finetuning on general retrieval, so they carry all the late-interaction structure with none of the general-purpose tuning that domain training then has to undo. The finished checkpoints, by contrast, barely moved or even regressed, at every learning rate I tried.</p><p>So, if the model family you like publishes a pre-supervised checkpoint, start there. If not, a fresh projection on a strong retrieval-pretrained backbone is a close runner-up. Continuing from a fully finished checkpoint is the weakest option for domain adaptation, despite being the most natural-feeling one.</p><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#dataset"> </a> Dataset</h2><p>The <a href="https://sbert.net/docs/package_reference/multi_vector_encoder/trainer.html"><code>MultiVectorEncoderTrainer</code></a> uses <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/datasets/main/en/package_reference/main_classes#datasets.Dataset"><code>datasets.Dataset</code></a> or <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/datasets/main/en/package_reference/main_classes#datasets.DatasetDict"><code>datasets.DatasetDict</code></a> instances for training and evaluation. You can load data from the <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets">Hugging Face Datasets Hub</a> or use local data in whatever format you prefer (e.g. CSV, JSON, Parquet, Arrow, or SQL).</p><p><strong>Note:</strong> Lots of public datasets that work out of the box with Sentence Transformers have been tagged with <code>sentence-transformers</code> on the Hugging Face Hub, so you can easily find them on <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets?other=sentence-transformers">https://huggingface.co/datasets?other=sentence-transformers</a>. Consider browsing through these to find ready-to-go datasets that might be useful for your tasks, domains, or languages.</p><h3><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#data-on-the-hugging-face-hub"> </a> Data on the Hugging Face Hub</h3><p>You can use the <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/datasets/main/en/package_reference/loading_methods#datasets.load_dataset"><code>load_dataset</code></a> function to load data from datasets on the Hub:</p><pre>from datasets import load_dataset

train_dataset = load_dataset(&quot;tomaarsen/miriad-4.4M-split&quot;, split=&quot;train&quot;)

print(train_dataset)

Simple (query, relevant passage) pairs like these are the easiest retrieval training data to collect for your own domain, and as you&#x27;ll see, they&#x27;re all you need.</p><h3><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#local-data"> </a> Local Data</h3><p>You can also use <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/datasets/main/en/package_reference/loading_methods#datasets.load_dataset"><code>load_dataset</code></a> for loading local data in common file formats:</p><pre>from datasets import load_dataset

dataset = load_dataset(&quot;csv&quot;, data_files=&quot;my_file.csv&quot;)

dataset = load_dataset(&quot;json&quot;, data_files=&quot;my_file.json&quot;)</pre><p>And if your local data requires pre-processing, you can use <a href="https://huggingface.co/docs/datasets/main/en/package_reference/main_classes#datasets.Dataset.from_dict"><code>datasets.Dataset.from_dict</code></a> to initialize your dataset with a dictionary of lists:</p><pre>from datasets import Dataset

queries = []
documents = []



dataset = Dataset.from_dict({
    &quot;query&quot;: queries,
    &quot;document&quot;: documents,
})</pre><h3><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#dataset-format"> </a> Dataset Format</h3><p>It is important that your dataset format matches your loss function (or that you choose a loss function that matches your dataset format). Verifying whether a dataset format works with a loss function involves two steps:</p><ol><li>If your loss function requires a <em>Label</em> according to the <a href="https://sbert.net/docs/multi_vector_encoder/loss_overview.html">Loss Overview</a> table, then your dataset must have a <strong>column named &quot;label&quot; or &quot;score&quot;</strong>. This column is automatically taken as the label.</li><li>All columns not named &quot;label&quot; or &quot;score&quot; are considered <em>Inputs</em> according to the <a href="https://sbert.net/docs/multi_vector_encoder/loss_overview.html">Loss Overview</a> table. The number of remaining columns must match the number of valid inputs for your chosen loss. The names of these columns are <strong>irrelevant</strong>, only the <strong>order matters</strong>.</li></ol><p>There are two multi-vector specific conventions on top of this:</p><ul><li>Positional query and document assignment: the first column is embedded as the <em>query</em> and all following columns as <em>documents</em>, regardless of the column names. This default can be overridden per column via the standard <code>router_mapping</code> training argument.</li><li>Knowledge distillation format: one column per candidate document, i.e. <code>(query, document_1, ..., document_N, scores)</code> where <code>scores</code> is a list of N teacher scores per row. For KD datasets that store query and document <em>IDs</em> alongside separate text datasets (e.g. <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/lightonai/ms-marco-en-bge">lightonai/ms-marco-en-bge</a>), you can use <a href="https://sbert.net/docs/package_reference/util.html#sentence_transformers.util.dataset.resolve_ids"><code>resolve_ids</code></a> to resolve the IDs to texts on the fly.</li></ul><h2><a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/train-multi-vector-encoder#loss-function"> </a> Loss Function</h2><p>Loss functions quantify how well a model performs for a given batch of data, allowing an optimizer to update the model weights to produce more favourable (i.e., lower) loss values. The right loss function for your task depends on the data you have and what you&#x27;re trying to achieve. You can find a full list of options in the <a href="https://sbert.net/docs/multi_vector_encoder/loss_overview.html">Loss Overview</a>.
